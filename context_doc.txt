1. BACKGROUND — WHAT IS NuR2
-----------------------------
NuR2 ("Nutrient Recovery Resilience") is a watershed-sustainability venture
(nur2.org) focused on America's agricultural nutrient runoff problem.

Core thesis:
- Billions of pounds of valuable nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) leave
  farm fields every year via runoff, instead of staying in the soil.
- Once in waterways, these nutrients feed harmful algal blooms, degrade
  water quality, raise municipal water treatment costs, and stress
  entire watersheds and fisheries.
- Historically, water quality efforts focused downstream (treatment
  plants, lake cleanups, stormwater infrastructure) or on "point source"
  pollution (factories, sewage — addressed by the 1970s Clean Water Act).
  Non-point source agricultural runoff was never solved at scale.
- NuR2's pitch: treat this as an infrastructure problem, not a farming
  failure. Build systems that intercept nutrients AT THE FIELD EDGE
  before they reach waterways, recover them as a reusable resource, and
  reinvest the value back into farm/watershed resilience.
- Model: "Capture -> Recover -> Return"
- AI angle: use AI-powered monitoring and watershed modeling to find the
  highest-leverage interception points and to verify/measure nutrient
  reduction outcomes (important for credibility with regulators,
  investors, and farm partners).

The site targets four audiences: farmers (nutrients as recoverable
assets, not losses), communities (lower treatment costs, cleaner
drinking water), regulators (measurable/verified reduction outcomes),
and conservation groups (healthier watersheds).


2. RELATED PROGRAM DISCOVERED — ANGLER SCIENCE DRIFTLESS AREA PROGRAM
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
While researching potential data sources, we found:
Angler Science Driftless Area Program — run by Trout Unlimited (TU) in
partnership with MobileH2O, LLC (mobileh2o.com/driftlessprogram)

What it is:
- A citizen-science water quality monitoring program covering the
  "Driftless Area" (unglaciated, hilly trout-stream region spanning
  parts of Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, and Illinois).
- Anglers use a smartphone app called WiseH2O plus physical test kits to
  record stream-side water quality readings while fishing: nitrate,
  nitrite, orthophosphate, pH, hardness, alkalinity, water temperature,
  clarity, water level, and visible disturbances (bank erosion, algal
  blooms, livestock in water, pipe/drain outflow, fish barriers, trash,
  fish kills).
- Scale: 2,319 observations from 2019-2024 (442 in 2024 alone), growing
  toward a goal of 3,000+ by 2025.
- Participants: anglers from 14 TU chapters, plus biologists/resource
  agents from US Fish & Wildlife Service, and the Wisconsin, Minnesota,
  and Iowa Departments of Natural Resources.
- Funded/supported by: TU's Coldwater Conservation Fund, TU Driftless
  Area Restoration Effort (TUDARE), USFWS, National Fish and Wildlife
  Foundation, National Fish Habitat Partnership.
- Output: a public map/dashboard, a "Driftless Area Scorecard"
  summarizing conditions and restoration opportunities, and downloadable
  raw data (this is the file we now have).

Why this is relevant to NuR2:
- Free, multi-year, geotagged water quality dataset across an
  agriculturally-adjacent watershed region.
- A built-in, trusted local network (TU chapters + state DNRs + USFWS)
  that could serve as a partner, pilot-region entry point, or
  outcome-verification partner.
- A ready-made "before/after" narrative structure that matches NuR2's
  own site messaging.

4. DATA QUALITY ISSUES / LIMITATIONS WE IDENTIFIED
-----------------------------------------------------
Important — found by direct inspection of the file, not assumption:

a) TWO NITRATE COLUMNS, ONE IS UNUSABLE
   - "NO3 Bin (5n1) (ppm)" — only 43 valid rows, and the values are
     nonsensical (negative numbers, range -116 to +148). This is a
     coding/parsing artifact from an older test-kit format. DO NOT USE.
   - "NO3 Bin (2n1) (ppm)" — 1,880 valid rows, sensible range (0-20 ppm,
     matching real nitrate test kit bins). USE THIS COLUMN.

b) SCREENING-KIT ACCURACY, NOT LAB-GRADE
   Angler-collected test kits give coarse "bin" readings, not precise
   lab measurements. Fine for flagging problem areas, not for precise
   load calculations.

c) SPATIAL BIAS TOWARD ACCESSIBLE FISHING SPOTS
   Observations cluster where anglers like to fish (public access,
   known trout streams) — not necessarily where nutrient loading is
   objectively worst. Areas with bad runoff but no nearby fishable
   water are likely underrepresented.

d) TEMPORAL BIAS / NO STORM-EVENT DATA
   Sampling is irregular, tied to when anglers happen to fish — skewed
   toward fishing season (spring/fall). Most nutrient runoff spikes
   happen during storm events, which this dataset largely misses.

e) NO PAIRED STREAMFLOW/DISCHARGE DATA
   The file has concentration (ppm/ppb) but not flow volume. Actual
   nutrient LOAD = concentration x flow. A high reading in a low-flow
   trickle may matter less than a moderate reading in high flow — we
   can't calculate true load from this file alone.

f) NO SOURCE ATTRIBUTION
   A high nitrate/phosphate reading doesn't tell us WHO caused it.
   Possible sources: row-crop fertilizer runoff, livestock operations,
   septic systems, municipal wastewater, golf courses, etc. Farm
   proximity on a map is not proof of causation.

g) SINGLE-POINT READINGS VS. PERSISTENT PROBLEMS
   One bad reading could be a storm event, temporary spill, or
   measurement error. We should prioritize watersheds with REPEATED
   high readings over time, not isolated red dots.

Net conclusion: this dataset is a strong SCREENING / PRIORITIZATION
layer, not a precise site-selection tool. It narrows hundreds of miles
of watershed down to a manageable shortlist of watersheds worth deeper
investigation — it should not be used alone to pick exact infrastructure
sites or specific farms.


4B. PRIOR ART — AN EXISTING TOOL ALREADY DOES PART OF THIS (IMPORTANT)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Before building our own siting tool from scratch, we should know this
exists:

THE AGRICULTURAL CONSERVATION PLANNING FRAMEWORK (ACPF)
- A free, USDA-developed (Agricultural Research Service + NRCS) ArcGIS
  toolbox, built specifically to identify site-specific locations for
  conservation practices — including woodchip bioreactors, phosphorus
  removal structures, saturated buffers, and constructed wetlands — at
  the FIELD and EDGE-OF-FIELD level within a watershed.
- Works at HUC12 watershed scale (same unit our data is already tagged
  with). "Core Data" packages exist for over 50,000 agricultural HUC12
  watersheds across the US.
- Uses high-resolution elevation (LiDAR), soils, and FIELD BOUNDARY data
  to hydro-condition terrain, determine which specific fields are most
  prone to contribute runoff to streams, and recommend exactly where a
  bioreactor, buffer, or wetland would be most effective.
- Already in active use across the Corn Belt, including Driftless-area
  states, coordinated through an "ACPF National Hub" (Iowa Water Center,
  Iowa State University GIS Facility, North Central Region Water
  Network, University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension).
- Free to download; requires ArcGIS and roughly two days of training to
  use effectively (built for GIS-literate watershed planners, not
  self-service for farmers or the public).

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR US:
- This is the closest existing analog to "Step 3-5" of our pipeline
  (siting conservation practices using watershed-scale geospatial data).
  We should not reinvent this from zero.
- Strategic options: (a) use ACPF's own Core Data / outputs directly for
  our candidate watersheds instead of rebuilding field-level hydrology
  from scratch, (b) build NuR2 as a layer ON TOP of ACPF-style siting —
  adding the AI-driven prioritization across many watersheds at once,
  the continuous verification/monitoring layer, and the economic model
  — none of which ACPF itself provides, or (c) at minimum, benchmark our
  own outputs against ACPF's recommendations for credibility.
- This reinforces the reframe above: the siting methodology isn't a
  blank space either. NuR2's differentiation is in doing this at
  portfolio/multi-watershed scale with AI-driven prioritization, ongoing
  verified measurement, and a farmer economic incentive — not in
  inventing field-level siting logic that already exists.

6. GEOGRAPHIC / HYDROLOGICAL CONTEXT (Downstream Connectivity)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
The HUC12 codes already embedded in the data are part of USGS's
official NESTED watershed hierarchy:
   HUC12 (smallest) -> HUC10 -> HUC8 -> HUC6 -> HUC4 -> HUC2 (largest)

All watersheds in this dataset sit within the UPPER MISSISSIPPI RIVER
BASIN, which ultimately drains to the GULF OF MEXICO. This directly
connects to the same "Gulf dead zone" narrative referenced on NuR2's own
website (nutrient-driven hypoxic zones downstream of the Mississippi).

Practical implication: a nutrient problem identified in one small HUC12
sub-watershed (e.g., Little Volga River) doesn't stay contained — it
flows downstream through every nested watershed below it until reaching
the Mississippi mainstem. This downstream propagation can be precisely
traced and quantified using USGS's NHDPlus flowline/watershed dataset
(not yet pulled — see Next Steps).


7. PROJECT GOAL
-----------------
Use AI/ML plus public geospatial data to identify the watersheds most
likely to be sources of agricultural nutrient runoff — going beyond
simple "look at the map and guess" — and produce a prioritized shortlist
of candidate watersheds/sub-basins for NuR2 to investigate further for
capture-infrastructure siting, pilot programs, or partnerships.

Explicitly acknowledged: the citizen-science dataset alone is NOT
sufficient for precise infrastructure site selection. It is a
screening/prioritization layer. Final site selection requires
additional data layers (below) and ground-truthing.

IMPORTANT REFRAME — WHERE NuR2'S REAL DIFFERENTIATION IS:
Edge-of-field nutrient capture technology itself (woodchip bioreactors,
phosphorus removal structures, saturated buffers, constructed wetlands)
is NOT new — it is decades-old, USDA-recognized, and reasonably
effective when properly sited. We should stop framing this as
"inventing a new machine nobody else has." The real gap in the field,
and where NuR2 can actually differentiate, is:
  1. Systematic, AI-driven, watershed-scale SITING — most current
     installations happen through ad hoc government cost-share programs,
     not rigorous, data-driven optimization across an entire river basin.
  2. Continuous, VERIFIED measurement — most existing installations are
     not monitored in real time, so nobody can currently prove "this
     bioreactor removed X pounds of nitrogen this year." Building that
     measurement/verification layer is a genuine gap.
  3. An ECONOMIC MODEL that makes farmers want this — the tech works,
     adoption is low partly due to lack of financial incentive. A
     verified-outcome-based nutrient credit market (same logic as carbon
     credit markets) could be the actual unlock.
This is a more fundable, buildable, and honest pitch than "we invented
ocean-scale filtration for farms" — and still a legitimately big deal if
executed well, because scaling deployment of a known-good technology has
changed the world before (e.g., solar panels existed for decades before
cost/deployment innovations made them ubiquitous).


8. PROPOSED PIPELINE / NEXT STEPS (in order)
------------------------------------------------
STEP 1 — Clean & standardize source data [DONE]
  - Drop/ignore the broken "NO3 Bin (5n1)" column; use "NO3 Bin (2n1)".
  - Standardize disturbance flag encoding (-1/0/1).

STEP 2 — Watershed-level rollup by HUC12 [DONE, first pass]
  - Aggregate by HUC12, require minimum observation count for
    confidence, rank by severity (nitrate, phosphate, disturbance
    flags). Expand to a full ranked list and consider a
    persistence/confidence score (e.g., weight by observation count and
    consistency of high readings across different dates).

STEP 3 — Land-use overlay [NOT YET DONE]
  - Pull the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) — free, public,
    satellite-derived annual land cover raster — for the upstream
    drainage area of each candidate watershed.
  - Compute % row-crop agriculture vs. pasture vs. forest vs. urban for
    each watershed's contributing area.
  - This is the step that actually tests whether "farms are the likely
    cause" vs. other plausible sources (septic, livestock feedlots,
    municipal discharge, urban runoff).

STEP 4 — Downstream impact tracing [NOT YET DONE]
  - Use USGS NHDPlus (National Hydrography Dataset Plus) flowline and
    watershed boundary data to:
    a) Confirm/visualize the exact downstream flow path from each
       candidate HUC12 to the Mississippi River mainstem.
    b) Quantify how many downstream miles / how many downstream HUC12s
       are affected by each hotspot — used as a "leverage" weighting
       factor (fixing a bigger upstream problem matters more).

STEP 5 — Composite scoring [NOT YET DONE]
  - Combine: severity (nitrate/phosphate levels) + persistence
    (consistency over time/observations) + % agricultural land upstream
    + downstream impact weight, into a single composite score.
  - Produce a ranked shortlist of roughly 10-15 candidate watersheds.

STEP 6 — Identify the actual farms/fields [NOT YET DONE — KEY STEP]
  This is the step that turns "problem watershed" into "specific fields
  to approach." Do NOT use map proximity — use actual upstream drainage
  delineation (from Step 4's NHDPlus work) intersected with field
  boundary data. Concrete, real public data sources to use:

  a) USDA Crop Sequence Boundaries (CSB) — FREE, PUBLIC, no privacy
     restriction. Open-source, algorithm-derived field boundaries for
     the entire contiguous US, built from satellite imagery + historic
     Cropland Data Layer data, with crop rotation history per field.
     Contains NO ownership or personal identifying information — just
     the physical field boundary and what's been grown there. This is
     our primary source for "which fields, physically, sit in this
     drainage area, and are they row-crop agriculture."

  b) ACPF state field-boundary datasets — pre-built field boundary
     layers (with an "isAG" flag) already assembled for ACPF use,
     available for several Midwest states including Iowa, Illinois, and
     Minnesota (derived originally from FSA Common Land Unit data, with
     all ownership/personal info stripped out). Useful as a
     cross-check against CSB and because it's already structured for
     HUC12-based conservation planning.

  c) IMPORTANT PRIVACY CAVEAT — what we CANNOT get from public data:
     The true parcel-level ownership/operator data (USDA FSA's Common
     Land Unit / CLU) is CONFIDENTIAL and not public, specifically to
     protect individual farmer privacy. Public datasets (CSB, ACPF
     field layers) intentionally strip out who owns/farms each field.
     This means: our pipeline can tell us WHICH FIELDS (physical
     locations) are likely contributors — it cannot tell us WHO to call.

  d) Bridging that gap — actual farmer identification/outreach requires
     a local, human step: county assessor/parcel records (public in
     most counties, but not nationally standardized), local NRCS field
     office relationships, or — most promising given what we already
     have — TU chapter and state DNR contacts from Section 2, who often
     already have established relationships with landowners in these
     watersheds. Treat this as a partnership/outreach task, not a data
     task.

STEP 7 — Ground-truthing [NOT YET DONE — real-world phase]
  - Conduct site visits for top candidate fields identified in Step 6.
  - Engage local TU chapters / state DNRs / NRCS offices as on-the-ground
    partners for farmer introductions (see 6d above).
  - Deploy NuR2's own field-level sensors (paired flow + concentration
    monitoring) to validate the modeled hotspot before committing to any
    capital infrastructure siting decision.


9. WHY WE'RE MOVING THIS WORK TO CLAUDE CODE
-------------------------------------------------
Two concrete, practical reasons surfaced during the chat-based work:

a) NETWORK ACCESS LIMITATION
   The chat sandbox environment used so far only has network access to
   a short allowlist (GitHub, PyPI, npm, and similar package
   registries). It CANNOT reach usda.gov or usgs.gov, which means Steps
   3 and 4 above (Cropland Data Layer, NHDPlus) cannot be completed in
   that environment. Claude Code running locally has normal, unrestricted
   internet access.

b) NO PERSISTENT PROJECT ACROSS SESSIONS
   The chat sandbox resets between tasks — there's no lasting project
   folder to keep building scripts, downloaded datasets, and
   intermediate outputs across multiple work sessions. Claude Code
   provides a real local project directory (this "nur2" folder) that
   persists, supports iterative script development, and works well with
   version control.

This phase of the work — building a multi-step, reusable geospatial
data pipeline — is a better fit for Claude Code than continued chat-based
one-off analysis.
